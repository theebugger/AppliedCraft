FAQ

Direct answers to the questions Applied Craft is most often asked. Each answer is short on purpose, with a link to the essay where the argument is made in full.

What is taste?

Taste is the evaluative half of how good work happens. Not a sensibility you either have or don't, but a 9-step cognitive loop you can train: receive what's in front of you cleanly, interpret it against references and language, resolve into a verdict you'll defend. The discipline underneath is clearing your own instrument so the signal at each step is your real response, not a performed one.

Can taste be trained?

Yes. Taste is not innate; it is a sequence of mental actions, each individually trainable. Exposure across breadth and depth builds the reference library. Naming practice reduces the loss between feeling and language. The loop is self-bootstrapping: every cycle sharpens the inputs of the next. The harder part is not the steps but the discipline of clearing desire, pride, and ego from your perception.

What is creativity, and how is it different from taste?

Creativity is the generative half of how good work happens; taste is the evaluative half. They oscillate: creativity proposes, taste filters, then creativity proposes again with a sharpened sense of what's worth proposing. Creativity is a function of three independent components, jointly necessary: intake (what you're feeding it), processing style (how you work with it), and depth of operation (how far below the articulating surface you can reach). The weakest component caps the whole output.

Is intuition just unfinished analysis?

No. Intuition is a third mode of knowing, distinct from analysis and from luck. It integrates incommensurables. It draws on embodied knowledge that resists symbolic articulation. It is doing the cognitive work that gets attributed to taste or judgment when the real work is being done somewhere else. Intuition may be the one form of cognition AI cannot meaningfully approximate.

What is the most important skill in the AI era?

Judgment. Of the six human skills usually cited as AI-proof (creativity, communication, empathy, leadership, critical thinking, judgment), only judgment actually scales in demand. The others remain stable. The AI era forces decision-making into the foreground while making the mechanical parts of professional work cheap, which exposes judgment as the load-bearing variable. Investing deliberately in judgment is the rational career bet.

Will AI replace product managers?

AI will not take a PM's job. But it might prevent a new PM from ever learning to do the job well. New PMs growing up with AI assistance face a specific risk: the struggles AI eliminates are often the same struggles that build judgment. Old PMs face a different risk: leveraging AI as a fast typewriter rather than as judgment infrastructure. Both failures end at the same place: quality of thinking.

What is the AI judgment trap?

AI is a struggle-eliminator. It removes friction from drafting, analysis, and synthesis. The struggle it removes is, in many cases, the same struggle that develops judgment. New PMs trained on AI-augmented work may never develop the override muscle that comes from repeatedly making and correcting calls under uncertainty. The competence trap is real and accelerating, and it is largely invisible from the inside.

How should product managers use AI?

There are roughly four levels of leverage. Level 1 (most PMs): AI as a faster typewriter, same task with more output. Level 2: AI as research assistant. Level 3: AI as critic, used to stress-test thinking. Level 4: AI as judgment infrastructure that reshapes which problems are even askable. Most PMs operate at Level 1 and call it mastery. The work is in moving up the levels, not in writing better prompts at Level 1.
